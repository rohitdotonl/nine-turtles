## Why another reader

Social media is infinite, urgent and built to keep you there. The open web
is still full of good writing, from people who publish on their own sites,
and most of them offer a feed: a small file that lists what's new. nine
turtles reads those feeds for you and gives you somewhere calm to read them.

It's built around one question, asked of every detail: *does this make it
feel more like a book, or more like a feed?*

<ul class="points">
  <li>
    <h3>Reads like a book</h3>
    <p>A typeface made for long reading, generous margins, and your choice of text size, font and line width. Day and night versions of every page.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>It ends</h3>
    <p>Your unread list is finite. When it's empty, you're done. Older articles load only when you ask, never by scrolling forever.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Nothing moves unless you move it</h3>
    <p>New articles wait quietly until you refresh. No pop-ups, no counters ticking up, no animations.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Respects the people who write</h3>
    <p>It shows exactly what each feed shares, always credits and links the original, and checks feeds politely, on each site's own rhythm.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Stays yours</h3>
    <p>Your feeds come in and go out as a standard OPML file, and your starred articles export whenever you like. Nothing locks you in.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <h3>Works everywhere</h3>
    <p>On a computer, a tablet or a phone. It installs to your home screen, and syncs with apps like Reeder and NetNewsWire.</p>
  </li>
</ul>

## What it will never do

Some things are left out on purpose, and stay out:

<div class="never">

- Notifications of any kind, and badges, streaks or reading stats
- Infinite scroll, or an algorithm deciding what you see
- Likes, comments, followers or other social features
- Scraping publishers' websites for more than their feed shares
- Ads, or tracking what you read

</div>
