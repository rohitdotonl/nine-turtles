# nine turtles: the owner's review

From a review held with Rohit on 3 November 2026. The reviewer took the
owner's chair and asked what Steve Jobs, going by his principles and not
his image, would do with nine turtles. Like everything in `docs/RnD/`, it's
research and a working plan, not a rule. The changes to the app that came
out of it go through the usual process (`AGENTS.md` → "How a change
happens").

**The sentence everything was judged against** (Rohit's reason for
building nine turtles, in the review's words): *nine turtles turns the
strangers you read into people you write to, and some of them into
friends. Reading is how you meet them. Writing back is how it starts.*

## How to make it better, every week

Don't ask an agent to remove each friction the moment you feel it. That's
how nine turtles reached 41 feature sections in two weeks: every annoyance
became a fix, and every fix became a setting. A friction is a symptom. Fix
it on the spot and you treat the symptom, add a little complexity, and
never see the cause.

### Every day: use it, don't fix it (one hour)

- **45 minutes of real reading,** the way you'd use it if you'd never built
  it. Half of it on your phone. No Settings, no testing, no agents.
- **Write back when something moves you.** That's the job nine turtles
  exists for, so it's the part to use most.
- **Keep a friction log,** one line each, the moment it happens, in a note
  on your phone: *what I was trying to do · what happened · how it felt.*
  For example: "Wanted to write to Kev · it asked for his email · I gave up
  and closed it."
- **15 minutes at the end:** reread today's lines and ask "why?" of each,
  then "why?" of the answer, until you reach something about people rather
  than a button. For example: "I gave up writing to Kev." Why? "It asked
  for his email." Why did that stop me? "I didn't know it, and finding it
  meant leaving the essay." Why does that matter? "The feeling that made me
  want to write faded while I searched." The real problem isn't a missing
  field: it's that the moment to write is short, and anything that breaks
  it loses the letter. That's what to fix.

### Ask why until you reach people

A line in the friction log is usually about a button. The real problem is
usually about a person. So don't stop at the first answer: keep asking
"why?" until the answer is about what a person felt or lost, not about
the screen.

- "I gave up writing to Kev." **Why?** It asked for his email.
- **Why did that stop me?** I didn't know it, and finding it meant leaving
  the essay.
- **Why does that matter?** The feeling that made me want to write faded
  while I searched.

The real problem isn't a missing field: the moment you want to write is
short, and anything that interrupts it loses the letter. Fix that, and
many button-level frictions disappear together. Fix only the button, and
the next one appears.

### Once a week: decide (one sitting, Friday)

1. **Group the week's lines.** The same friction three times is a real
   problem. Once is a mood.
2. **Choose at most three,** and only ones on the main path: find a person,
   read, write back. Everything else waits.
3. **First ask: can we remove something instead of adding something?** Most
   frictions are fixed by taking away a step, a word or a choice.
4. **Write each one as what the reader should feel,** not as a feature:
   "Writing to Kev takes one press" rather than "add an email finder".
5. **Hand them to an agent** in a fresh thread, try the result on the Mac,
   and ship only what feels better in your own hands.

### Once a month: see it fresh

- **A brand-new account,** no feeds, ten minutes. There's no other way to
  see the first five minutes.
- **Watch one other person** use it, on a call, without helping. Their
  hesitation is worth a week of your log.
- **Look at one number:** how many letters did you write this month? If
  it's going down, nothing else that shipped matters.

### Three rules for every change

- **One in, one out.** Before adding anything, name what it replaces or
  what goes.
- **Nothing ships that you haven't used for a day.**
- **No new settings.** If a choice is hard, the owner makes it, not the
  reader.

### Removing without being sure

When something is removed, you don't need to be sure nobody wants it.
Readers can write to Rohit, and he answers every email. If three people
ask for something back, it's a real need, and their emails say exactly
why they want it. That's a better reason to build something than a guess
made before anyone used it. (The review's example: rules lose their
"star it" and "mark it as read" actions, the author field, folder targets
and the title-or-text choice, and keep "Hide articles about [words] from
[everyone / this person]".)

## The owner's to-do list

Things only Rohit can do. The changes to build went to the coordinator for
a plan; nothing is built until Rohit says go.

### This week

- [ ] **Write to five people through the app,** start to finish: open their
  article, press Write back, send. Note every moment it got in the way.
- [ ] **Fix your own broken feeds,** and note what made each one hard.
- [ ] **Set the price.** The review's suggestion: $8 a month or $80 a year,
  and $60 a year for the first 100 readers for as long as they stay. Any
  number unblocks Dodo, the website and the launch.
- [ ] **Fix the website's contradictions:** Questions says "Not yet for
  everyone" and that podcasts are "a link to the episode"; About says the
  look "takes its lead from Apple Books"; Questions says "Never" to
  notifications beside the weekly email; Pricing says "to be decided"
  while sign-up is open.
- [ ] **Start the friction log** (above).

### Starter people, replacing the five starter feeds

Today "New to feeds? Start here" suggests The Marginalian, The Public
Domain Review, kottke.org, Austin Kleon and NASA Image of the Day: mostly
publications, under a headline that says "Read people".

- [ ] **Pick five people** for the same place, same number, none ticked.
  Each one:
  - writes regularly and well, on their own site;
  - is writable: a contact page, or an email in their feed;
  - has said yes when asked: "May I suggest you to new readers of nine
    turtles?" (a real letter from Rohit);
  - and together they're a mix: not all tech, not all one country.
- [ ] **If fewer than five say yes,** show none, and link to where people
  find each other instead: Bear Blog's discover page, ooh.directory,
  blogroll.org.

### Within 30 days

- [ ] **Put nine turtles in front of ten ideal people,** by hand, starting
  with readers who have already written to you.
- [ ] **Watch one of them use it** on a video call, without helping. Write
  down where they hesitate.
- [ ] **Make a fresh account** and go through the first ten minutes as a
  stranger. Repeat monthly.

### Later

- [ ] **Say plainly how nine turtles is made** (for example, "I design it;
  AI agents write the code"), since the repository is public.
- [ ] **Decide on syncing with Reeder and NetNewsWire** after 50 readers:
  does anyone use it, and do they still write back?
- [ ] **Watch one number:** letters started, per reader, per month (a total
  in the owner report, never per person). Needs building when Rohit says
  so.
- [ ] **The six-month test:** 50 devoted readers, meaning they pay, come
  back most weeks without being asked, and have written to at least one
  person. A public launch only after that.
